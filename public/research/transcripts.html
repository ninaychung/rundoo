Carpet Place — Edison, NJ

Store manager, ~5 years · In person, ~45 min, plus two follow-ups · Included a walkthrough of the paper order forms, the installer copy and the filing cabinet · Owner unavailable

Two locations, 26 years, around 15 installers. Five to six customers a week at roughly $6,000 average. 80–85% close rate. 80% residential.

On scheduling

"We have a book. So once the job goes in the book, I would send them the work order. And then they would go do the job."

Orders are handwritten on paper forms with the purchase form stapled to the back, filed in a cabinet under the scheduled install date, then archived when the job is done. There is no customer list.

On the installers

"They're really subcontractors — if someone asks, we say they work for us, because they do work for us. Like, they're not here one day and gone the next. They've been here three, four years."

Contrasted explicitly with big-box hiring: "Like Home Depot just hires people, sends them out on a job, and then sometimes you never see them again. But our contractors have been here for years."

On how installers are paid

"If they do 100 yards, they get paid for 100 yards to rip up the old, 100 yards to install the new. And then steps — they get extra for steps. So everything has a price. So whatever they do, that's how they get paid."

Wall base and cove base are extra. Furniture moves are extra. Paid by the foot, never hourly. A single line item covers wall base and cove base together, because it spans metal, vinyl and wood.

On billing

"And then every week they turn in a bill. Of all the jobs they did, how many feet, how much they charge a foot, and then the total."

One invoice per installer covering the whole week, across every job.

On who writes the invoice

"You guys don't create this invoice. They do." Asked whether the installers use software for it: "They just type it up." Payment goes out by check.

On large jobs with multiple crews

"There's one main boss, so he has, like, four or five different crews. So if he has a big job, he'll bring all his crews with him. And then he would bill. They get paid by the day, his installers — he'll pay the installers."

The store deals only with the boss, never with individual crew members.

On what changes mid-job

Asked whether planned and actual often differ: "The majority of the time it's whatever it says here." If an installer forgets how many yards he did, he calls and asks.

The exception: "Say the floor was uneven — we can't tell that until we rip up the carpet. It's called leveler. It's like a cement, and they would put that down to make the floor even." Mostly vinyl and wood; sometimes carpet, though a sloped floor is usually visible through carpet.

On why they have no system

Her preferred system is a flooring-specific programme she used at a previous employer — it tracked stock, calculated prices, transferred data and printed orders. She couldn't recall the name.

The owner looked into it and was quoted roughly $6,000–7,000 upfront plus monthly fees. He judged it too expensive. She has raised going digital with him; he declined.

On ordering

"I like the phone because I like talking to someone to make sure it's really there." She orders direct from the mill — Mohawk, Shaw, Canopy, Masland, Dixie Home — rather than through a distributor, and prefers the call so backorders surface in conversation rather than later.

On checking stock and price

The only computer use in the store is the manufacturers' dealer portals. With the customer present she checks the style, colour, width, fibre, unit price per square yard and live stock, then quotes from it.

Photographed: the Dixie Group / Masland portal under the store's own dealer account.

On negotiation

Negotiation happens with the customer "90% of the time." There is a set percentage she can come down. On jobs over roughly 1,000 square feet she calls the manufacturer rep for a job-specific discount, then beats the competing quote — winning roughly nine times in ten.

On manufacturer reps

"For each product, there's a sales rep. Some of them come in once a week. Some of them come in every two weeks."

Asked how she hears about new technology or products: "He came in and he said, this is peel and stick tile, and you could go over anything with it except carpet. You could go over wood, you could go over ceramic tile, and it just sticks to anything. So he'll come in and tell me everything about it. That's how I learn about the new products."

On samples

Asked whether they track every sample in the store: "No, no, no. They come and go. The sales rep will come in and pull out the discontinued ones, and we just throw them out, and then new ones come, like every other day."

Sample collections are bought — around $1,000 for a set of 30 to 40 boards, purchased at an annual market in Atlantic City — and rebated once the collection reaches a sales threshold, around $5,000.

On buying groups and peers

Not a member of any buying group. Had never heard of CCA Global. Does not keep in contact with other flooring store owners in the area.

On tile

They dropped tile entirely. It's harder to estimate — size, shape, diagonal layouts, edging. Those customers now go to Home Depot, Floor & Decor or Flemington.

Materials. Carpet Place supplies flooring, wall base, transition strips, glue and pads. The installer supplies everything else, including tackless strip. Materials are billed from the manufacturer to the store; the customer's bill combines materials and installation.

West Carpets — Rahway, NJ

40-year industry veteran, formerly owner of her own commercial flooring company, out of retirement to help the new owners · In person, ~40 min, with project drawings and sample books

Roughly 99% commercial. Team of five. Projects include Newark Airport Terminal A, Honda dealerships, a golf club, a ~1,000-unit senior building and a New York penthouse.

On how commercial work arrives

An architect selects every finish — walls, HVAC, flooring. The package goes out to bid to four or five construction companies. West Carpets bids to the general contractor as one subcontractor among many; the GC adds margin and bids the whole job. If their GC wins, they get the work. If not, it goes elsewhere.

After award, submittals verify that what was specified is what's being supplied.

On revisions

Projects run over a year on ground-up builds, and plans change constantly. Her live example: product specified in January was discontinued by September, and she had to email the client to ask whether to buy the old product or switch.

On installers

"Our guys work for us." Installers are employees, not subcontractors, driven by prevailing-wage public work where the state is strict. Formerly a union shop.

On sample racks and shows

"We used to be, but we're not anymore. We're not because we don't do that much residential, it costs too much, and they charge you for everything. They nickel-and-dime these retailers — they pay a lot for these, because of updates."

On trade shows: there's an annual market in Vegas, but it's mostly retail-focused and "very few people go anymore." She prefers smaller local shows, warehouse shows and installation clinics held at the store.

On product structure and pricing

The style is the SKU, with many colours beneath it — a system would need the style plus every colour in the line. Pricing comes from the rep by email, based on quantity, rather than from a published list. A retail store typically works with only one or two manufacturers.

On estimating

Waste figured at five percent, sometimes five to ten, complicated by multi-colour designs and box quantities — a box might be 45 square feet. Floor preparation is a major cost line: self-levelling compound, anti-fracture membrane, thinset. Transition strips run to hundreds of profiles across more than twenty finishes in metal and vinyl, and transition heights must meet ADA requirements, which is federal law.

On a distributor that failed

She described, unprompted, a Secaucus flooring distributor whose new computer system failed in the 1990s, leaving them unable to generate invoices to bill stores, and which subsequently went bankrupt.

Verified independently: Kane Carpet, $90M in annual sales and 7,000+ clients, installed a $318,000 McDonnell Douglas system that went live in March 1989. Within days orders were being delivered multiple times, a $50,000 order was billed at $500, and warehouse inventory logs were wildly wrong. The company went bankrupt in August 1990 owing roughly $35M and sued for $37M; the vendor blamed the recession. Owner Alan Braunstein founded Worldwide Wholesale Floor Coverings in 1991. (NJBIZ)

On the independent segment

"Most of your mom and pops aren't gonna have any system — everything's done by hand still." And: "They're very cheap. Typically they start up in their house."

On retail versus commercial: a walk-in customer can cost you an hour while you're trying to get a bid out. A lot of the small independents are gone; Floor & Decor and Home Depot took the ground.

Standard Tile — Edison, NJ

Showroom manager · In person, with a walkthrough · Owner had just left · Referred by West Carpets

Family-owned, over a hundred years, five locations. Sells material only — no measuring, no installation. Recommends contractors; the customer's contractor supplies the measurements.

On inventory

Inventory is in the computer, not manual — twenty cases out, the count drops by twenty. Stock is visible across all five stores. Occasional manual counts serve as a check. Samples on the floor do not count as inventory; only full boxes waiting to be sold.

The point of sale is administered centrally from the head office in Totowa; the showroom manager couldn't speak to it directly.

On supplier systems

For at least half their manufacturers, business-to-business portals let them check whether stock sits in New Jersey, Virginia or Texas. Ordering goes through an online system and email, not by phone.

On pricing

Most prices are not marked on displays, and haven't been for a while. The reason is tariffs and fuel — diesel is over six dollars a gallon and trucks move everything, so costs move. Prices can change quarterly.

Corporate sets prices: wholesale cost runs through a formula to a retail price, with some wiggle room at the store to discount. Discounts go to homeowners, first responders, teachers, community members, and on volume — someone buying 2,000 square feet gets more than someone buying 20.

On colour lots

Manufacturers produce a set quantity; on a re-run the colour may not match. They have had products they liked discontinued because the colour couldn't be reproduced.

On sample displays

Manufacturers supply the display stands. Some give them free; some charge and refund once the store hits a sales threshold. Boards are rarely updated — mainly when a colour lot changes. Some displays have been up three years.

On sourcing

Tile is mostly European — Spain, Italy, Portugal — with some Vietnam, India and Turkey, and mosaics from China. Importers hold local warehouses, so stock is usually in-state with a three-to-seven-day lead time. Deliveries run twice a week between stores.

On Floor & Decor

The biggest threat. Lower prices, but she says lower quality — their goods are largely Chinese against Standard Tile's European — and some contractors refuse to install their product. The local cluster is known as the tile mile.

Royal Stone Cabinet & Tile — Flushing, NY

Family owner — daughter of the part-owners · Video call · Introduced through a mutual contact · Notes are summary, not verbatim

Tile, countertop and cabinet dealer with its own fabrication shop. 50% tile, 30% countertop, 20% cabinet. 70% residential; within that, roughly half homeowners and half contractors.

On operations

Thirty employees across fabrication, warehouse, sales, install and delivery. Five installers, a mix of employees and contractors. Estimators and installers are separate roles. Installer schedules are optimised by customer address for route efficiency.

Commercial margin runs around 15% markup; orders are typically above $10,000. Two weeks for residential, about a month for commercial. The commercial contact chain runs designer, then owner, then project manager.

On sourcing

Sources from Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Spain — deliberately not China, because of tariffs. Visits the factories twice a year to check quality, colour and style.

On buying ahead of demand

Orders are placed before customer demand, with small trial orders for new styles. Lead time runs two to three months from order to arrival. Inventory is reviewed and cleared monthly; slow-moving stock is pushed to contractors, who are less style-sensitive than homeowners. Colour batch mismatch is managed by warning customers upfront.

On product codes

The code listed for each tile is the supplier's item number, not an internal Royal Stone code. The supplier assigns it; Royal Stone uses it to identify the exact product when placing repeat orders, and it lets the supplier find the product in its own records. Brand logos and names don't appear on the website because they work with several different overseas factories.

On technology

WeChat, WhatsApp, Gmail, Excel and QuickBooks. No dedicated inventory or business management software. Her daily routine: check email, review inventory, check schedules, monitor fabrication, follow up on deliveries, serve walk-in customers.

Open to exploring software, but concerned it can't keep up with a one-to-two-month inventory turnover cycle.

Worldwide Flooring — Edison, NJ

Senior salesperson, in person, ~20 min, near closing on a Saturday · Second visit obtained a card from new business development

Five locations — Edison, Old Bridge, Lawrenceville, Fairfield and Paramus. Owners are a family, usually in Monday or Friday. Founded 1991 by Alan Braunstein, previously of Kane Carpet.

On positioning against big box

Home Depot sells flooring material; Worldwide is a flooring company. The difference is expertise plus guaranteed installation. Departments are organised by product — wood, vinyl, carpet, tile — plus kitchen cabinets and baths handled as custom projects with a dedicated CAD specialist.

On samples

"We're not a sample store." Samples must be ordered per customer — around five dollars for carpet, twenty for tile. This is the opposite approach to the carpet dealers, who buy full sample collections.

On labour

The estimators who go out to measure are outside contractors, not employees. So are the installers. Timeline runs one to six weeks. Some remnants are on hand; most goods are ordered.

Before ordering they need to know floor condition, doors, transitions and molding — which requires a site visit.

On traffic

Saturday traffic collapsed after the pandemic: around fifty customers on a Saturday before, around ten now. They also dropped sheet vinyl entirely, as quality declined and their manufacturers exited; vinyl plank replaced it.

On technology

On a second visit, new business development declined to discuss the company's technology or operations, describing the information as proprietary, and offered to take questions by email.

Observation: the dealers running on paper talked openly for 45 minutes. The five-location dealer with a working system treated its stack as competitive information.

Huey Ho — contractor and architect

Expert interview, not a store · Channel-level context · Secondhand on retail specifics — framing rather than primary evidence
On the wholesale layer

MSI is an importer and wholesaler of flooring, tile and countertops based in Edison, shipping nationwide, and sells to retailers, contractors and homeowners directly. Retailers get a lower price than homeowners — say ten dollars against twelve — leaving room to undercut MSI's own direct price.

Manufacturers can't store finished goods at the factory, so they ship continuously to wholesalers, who warehouse and distribute. If the wholesaler can't sell it, they discount to clear.

Many small retailers don't stock at all — the customer's order flows through to the wholesaler, who delivers direct. Large contractors, such as a hundred-unit apartment building, buy direct from manufacturers or wholesalers, bypassing retailers entirely. Home Depot's contractor membership offers online ordering, free delivery, credit and no sales tax.

On how specification works

Manufacturers market to architects and interior designers, often visiting firms with a local sales rep or retailer in tow. The architect specifies materials in the construction documents — brand, thickness — and the contractor sources accordingly. The architect typically recommends two or three suppliers; the contractor negotiates with the retailer or the rep.

Retailers serve as the local contact point: they refer contractors for installation and handle homeowner walk-ins. Platforms like Angi aggregate local contractors, taking twenty to thirty percent.

On who the repeat customers are

Small retailers don't track repeat customers. Homeowners rarely return; contractors are the repeat business.

On technology adoption — and his objection

Small retailers are one- and two-person operations with paper contracts and no meaningful software. Medium and larger retailers use basic software for order entry, payment tracking and accounting, with product data keyed in manually. There's no integration with wholesaler systems — two entirely separate stacks.

His view: flooring retail skews very small with low technology adoption. Small retailers carry products from many different companies, which makes a single integrated system hard to pitch, and they lack the staff to maintain a product database. He thinks the better fit sits at the wholesale or larger-retail level — though MSI-scale wholesalers may already be on enterprise systems, making mid-size retailers or regional wholesalers the realistic target.
