How the flooring market works

Rundoo serves independent supply stores across hardware, paint, lumber, farm and feed, and lawn and garden. This asks whether flooring is a viable next vertical.

Market size

And the distinction most reports get wrong

There are 17,834 US floor covering stores doing $61.5bn in 2026. Separately there are 118,405 flooring installer businesses, and most market reports fold the two together. Installers hold no inventory and place no purchase orders — the addressable dealer count is roughly a tenth of the headline figure. Growth is flat, under one percent a year, but evergreen.

Two differences matter strategically. In paint, hardware and lumber the incumbent systems are horizontal — Epicor, ECI, sold into many trades. In flooring they are flooring-native, and consolidating. And flooring is the only one of these verticals where the store owns the installation labour.

How the market works

Project retail, not counter retail

Revenue starts with an in-home measurement and ends with an installation. Almost nothing is bought off a shelf and carried home.

A note on the word "wholesale"

The industry uses it loosely, and three different things wear the label. Importer-wholesalers — MSI, Soho Studio, TimeFloor — buy from overseas factories, warehouse, and sell to retailers, contractors and homeowners alike. Distributor-owned retail — Standard Tile sells at retail but sits inside a distribution business, which is why it can see stock across five locations and into supplier systems. And retailers trading under a wholesale name — Worldwide operates as "Worldwide Wholesale Floor Coverings" but is a five-location retailer. The name is positioning, not structure.

The chain gets bypassed constantly. Large contractors buy direct from manufacturers; wholesalers sell direct to homeowners; Home Depot's contractor membership offers credit, delivery and no sales tax. The retailer is optional at several points.

Residential

Roughly a week and a half, around $6,000 average, five to six customers a week, eighty to eighty-five percent close. Negotiation happens on nine jobs in ten. On large jobs the store phones the manufacturer rep mid-quote for a discount and beats the competing number, winning about nine times out of ten.

Commercial

A different business entirely. An architect specifies every finish in the construction documents, the package goes out to four or five general contractors, and the flooring dealer bids to the GC as one subcontractor among many. If their GC loses the job, they lose it too. Projects run weeks to over a year and plans revise constantly — one dealer had product specified in January discontinued by September, mid-project.

Who owns which steps

Every business owns a contiguous stretch — what differs is where they start and stop

Owning more steps isn't better. Standard Tile owns fewer than anyone except the wholesalers, and is the most sophisticated operator interviewed.

Labour

Three models across four stores

Subcontracted — Carpet Place and Worldwide. Around fifteen installers, paid per foot and never hourly. They're described as subcontractors who nonetheless stay three or four years. Pay is itemised by task: rip up, install, steps, furniture moves, wall base. Everything has a price. Worldwide contracts out even the estimating.

Employed — West Carpets keeps its installers on the books, because prevailing-wage public work requires it.

Mixed — Royal Stone runs five installers across both, with estimators as a separate role and schedules routed by customer address.

Any system serving this market has to tolerate all three.

How the money moves back

The store never bills the crew — the crew bills the store, in a batch, a week later. One invoice covers every job that installer completed that week, priced by the foot with separate lines per task. And the installer writes it, not the store. They type it up themselves; payment goes out by check. On larger jobs the store deals only with a crew boss, who brings his own four or five crews and pays them by the day.

Reps, samples and catalogues

Where the industry's information actually travels

Manufacturer reps visit every week or two. They restock racks, pull discontinued boards, pitch new product, and negotiate spot discounts in the middle of a live quote. They are also the primary channel through which owners hear about anything new.

Sample displays are bought, not given. The common structure is that the dealer pays for a collection up front and is rebated once it hits a sales threshold — around $1,000 for thirty to forty boards, refunded at roughly $5,000 in sales. Standard Tile describes the same arrangement. Genuinely free racks exist but misfire: MSI supplied tile racks to a kitchen-and-bath business that doesn't sell flooring, and nothing ever sold. West Carpets dropped the programme entirely on cost. Worldwide takes the opposite position and keeps no samples at all. Nobody tracks samples as inventory.

Catalogue access varies by segment

For branded goods, manufacturers publish dealer pricing and live stock on portals — Carpet Place checks unit price per square yard and availability while the customer is standing there. Standard Tile can see manufacturer stock by state for about half its suppliers.

For imported goods there is no brand layer, but the product codes are the supplier's own item numbers, assigned by the factory and used by both sides on reorder. A catalogue exists — it just lives upstream.

For commercial, pricing arrives by email from the rep on a quantity basis rather than a published list. The structural challenge is depth: the style is the SKU, with every colour beneath it.

The software landscape

Two consolidations running at once — and neither describes the stores I visited

What the field actually shows

Carpet Place — entirely paper. No point of sale, no back office, no accounting software, no customer list. Orders filed by install date; scheduling in a book. The only computer use is checking stock on mill portals.

Royal Stone — WeChat, WhatsApp, Gmail, Excel, QuickBooks. Open to software, but worried it can't keep pace with a one-to-two-month turnover.

Standard Tile — a real system, with inventory visible across five stores.

Worldwide — declined to discuss it, calling it proprietary.

The refusal is itself data. The dealers running on paper talked for forty-five minutes; the one with a working system treated its stack as competitive information.

The owner priced a flooring system at six to seven thousand up front plus monthly, and said no. At roughly $1.5–1.8m in revenue, that's three tenths of one percent of sales. This is not an owner refusing technology — it's an owner who did the arithmetic.

Software risk here is not abstract

Worldwide's founder, Alan Braunstein, previously ran Kane Carpet, a Secaucus distributor doing $90m a year. A $318,000 McDonnell Douglas system went live in March 1989; within days orders were being delivered multiple times and inventory records were wrong. Kane went bankrupt in 1990 owing $35m and sued for $37m. The vendor blamed the recession, and the verdict isn't in the public record. Braunstein founded Worldwide in 1991. A forty-year veteran at another store named the episode independently.

Competitors and independents

Where the ground is actually being lost

Floor & Decor runs 281 stores across 39 states, roughly eight percent of the industry. But comparable store sales fell 2.1% while total sales rose 3.0% — all the growth comes from opening stores, not from existing ones. And they sell no carpet. Carpet Place dropped tile because it's hard to estimate — shape, diagonal layouts, edging — and sends those customers to Home Depot or Floor & Decor, while keeping carpet. The categories big box takes are the ones that sell over a counter.

Independents compete on price and on quality. Customers phone every store in the area. Dealers discount by volume, stock age and special groups, and ask manufacturers for job-specific discounts. One tile retailer stopped marking prices on displays altogether, because tariffs and fuel move costs quarterly. Standard Tile sells European tile and says some contractors refuse to install big-box product. Worldwide's framing: they sell flooring material, we're a flooring company.

Showroom traffic has collapsed. Saturdays once brought around fifty customers; now it's around ten.

Buying groups — CCA Global, Abbey, Alliance, NFA — pool purchasing power and sometimes dictate technology. Carpet Place isn't a member and had never heard of CCA, nor keeps contact with other owners. No buying-group member was interviewed. That's the largest gap in this research, and the question that would most change the go-to-market answer.

What entering would look like

Two paths, requiring different amounts of building

Don't compete on breadth. Cyncly owns the enterprise tier. At the independent end the incumbent is paper and the competitor is inertia. A forty-year veteran put it plainly: most mom-and-pops have no system at all, and everything is still done by hand.

Path one — land where the product already fits

The speculative importer-retailer. Royal Stone and Tile Loft buy months ahead, run warehouses, clear stock monthly, and manage all of it in spreadsheets. Inventory, purchasing, costs and catalogues are Rundoo's product today. The catalogue burden is lighter than it first appears, since supplier item numbers are stable enough to reorder against. Royal Stone is explicitly open to software. Lowest build cost, fastest first customer.

Path two — build the project layer, where the larger segment sits

Special-order retailers like Carpet Place hold no stock, so inventory and purchasing have little to attach to. Their pain is measure, quote, scheduling and installer reconciliation — none of which exists in the product today. This is the bigger population and the harder build, and it's where the accompanying prototype is aimed.

The wedge that connects them

Carpet Place supplies every installation material except tackless strip — wall base, transition strips, glue, pads — billed manufacturer to store to customer. Its second location stocks moldings, metal and reducers because contractors need material same-day. That inventory behaves exactly like a paint or hardware store's, even at a dealer that otherwise carries nothing. And contractors are the repeat customers; homeowners rarely return, which makes charge accounts and customer records more valuable than dealers realise.

What would make me wrong

  • If dealers won't buy anything that doesn't own the job, path one stalls and the real cost is the full project layer.
  • If ingesting from many small overseas suppliers is harder than from a handful of US mills, the importer segment gets harder.
  • If $6–7k was a proxy for "I don't want this," a lower price won't help.
Limitations. Six interviews, one metropolitan area, only one with an owner and that by video. No buying-group member reached. No pain quantified in hours or dollars. Royal Stone notes are summaries rather than a transcript.
