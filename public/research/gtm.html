Field playbook: selling into flooring

Written for the rep who has to walk through the door. Part one is what these owners actually read, attend and trust. Part two is what to do about it.

The one-line version. These owners don't read trade press, don't go to national shows, and don't talk to each other. They listen to the manufacturer rep who walks in every week, and they buy in the dead season. Plan around that.

What they read, watch and attend

Answering the three questions directly, from the field

What they read or watch: almost nothing

No owner named a publication, a podcast or a channel. The trade press exists — FloorDaily, Floor Covering News, Floor Covering Weekly — but nobody I spoke to mentioned it unprompted, and I didn't ask the question directly enough to claim otherwise. Treat this as unverified rather than as a finding.

What did come up: dealer groups on Facebook, and manufacturer-run product clinics held inside the store. Neither is a media buy.

What conferences they attend: regional, not national

  • An annual manufacturer market in Atlantic City, where new collections launch and dealers buy their sample sets for the year. This is the one every carpet dealer mentioned. Exact name not obtained — worth pinning down.
  • Local warehouse shows and in-store installation clinics. The forty-year veteran prefers these to anything national.
  • The Vegas market — Surfaces — exists and is retail-focused, but her view was that very few people go anymore.

National shows reach manufacturers, distributors and the larger dealers. They are a partnership channel, not a way to reach a two-store independent.

Which reps they trust: all of them, weekly

This is the finding that matters. Every product line has its own manufacturer rep, and they come in weekly or every two weeks. They restock the sample racks, pull discontinued boards, walk the owner through new product, and get called mid-quote to authorise a discount on a large job.

Asked how she learns about anything new, the store manager described a rep arriving with peel-and-stick tile and explaining what it could go over. That is the information channel for this industry.

Manufacturers already push technology into these stores at no cost — several supply room visualizers free. So a rep bringing a piece of software is not a foreign idea to the relationship.

The untested question. Would a dealer take a rep's recommendation on software, or only on product? Nobody was asked. It is the first thing to test, and the whole channel rests on it.

Who they don't listen to: each other

Carpet Place is not in a buying group, had never heard of CCA Global, and does not keep in contact with other flooring store owners. If that's typical, peer referral is far weaker here than in most trades, and the rep channel matters more by default.

The playbook

Six steps, in order

1

Build the list

Four sources, cheapest first.

  • Ask a territory rep for their route. A single Mohawk or Shaw rep can name every independent dealer in a county — it's their weekly drive. One phone call beats any directory.
  • Buying group dealer locators. CCA, Abbey and Alliance publish member finders so consumers can locate stores. Free, geocoded, pre-qualified.
  • Importer dealer programmes. Wholesalers who supply sample stands to qualified dealers know exactly who hits volume minimums.
  • Drive the corridors. See step two.

No website is a buying signal, not an obstacle. One forty-year family business in the list has no site at all. Don't filter them out — they're the best prospects.

2

Work the corridors

Flooring dealers cluster near wholesale infrastructure, not near retail traffic. A rep can cover six stores in a day without leaving one road.

Assign territory by language where it matters. In some corridors the business is conducted in Mandarin or Cantonese, and a rep who can hold the conversation gets a meeting where one who can't gets a polite no.

College Point Blvd, Flushing NY

Mandarin / Cantonese

Dense tile, stone and cabinet showrooms, many direct importers. Open early — 7 or 8am is contractor hours.

Route 1, Edison NJ

English

Locally called the tile mile. Multi-location dealers and distributor-owned retail sit within a few miles.

Route 22, Green Brook NJ

English

Three family tile and carpet dealers inside two miles, one trading sixty years.

Northern Blvd, Queens NY

English / Korean

Long-established carpet dealers, several running their own install crews.

3

Get in the door

Walk in. Don't call, don't email. Across thirty-plus businesses contacted for this research: emails produced zero replies, phone calls produced callbacks that never came, and every single substantive conversation came from walking through the door.

Tuesday to Thursday, mid-morning. The showroom is empty and the owner is usually in the building. Avoid Saturday — that's their biggest retail day. Avoid Monday morning, when they're digging out from the weekend.

Ask for the owner by name. Look it up first. If they're out, ask the one useful question — when is he usually in — and come back.

4

Open on the paper, not the product

Do not lead with features, and do not lead with a system. Lead with what you can see on their counter.

Opening "I'm not here to sell you anything today. I work with independent supply stores — paint, hardware, lumber — and we're trying to understand flooring. Can I ask how you keep track of your installs?"

Then listen for the tells. Any of these means you're in the right store:

  • A book or a whiteboard for the install schedule
  • A filing cabinet organised by install date
  • "I'd have to look that up" when asked about a past customer
  • Handwritten invoices from installers stacked on a desk
  • Two or more locations with no shared view of stock
When they describe the paper "So if a customer from three years ago called tomorrow — how would you find what you sold them?"

This lands because the answer is usually memory. It's the moment the cost becomes visible without you asserting anything.

5

Know who wants it and who signs

These are often not the same person, and getting the order wrong kills the deal.

At one store the manager had already pushed the owner to modernise and been told no. She feels the cost daily — the book, the cabinet, the weekly reconciliation. He signs the cheque and doesn't.

Sequence: reach the manager or the person at the counter first and let them describe the pain. Arm them with the numbers. Then reach the owner — ideally through someone he already trusts, not cold.

Watch for the moments when an owner is most open: a second location opening, a handover to the next generation, a rebrand, a new partner coming in, or a vendor telling them their current system is being retired.

6

Time it for the dead season

Flooring is quiet from December through February. That is exactly when an owner has the time to sit through a demonstration and move off paper, and when the disruption costs least. October and November are the pre-holiday rush — don't bother. March through November is peak.

What they'll say back

Every one of these came up in the field

"It's too expensive."

The most common and the most concrete. One owner priced a flooring system at six to seven thousand up front plus monthly and turned it down — on a business doing roughly $1.5–1.8m a year.

The up-front number is what gets quoted back at you, not the subscription. Lead with the first-year cost and be specific about it. Don't argue that it's cheap relative to revenue; he already did that arithmetic and still said no.

"We've done it this way for twenty-six years."

Don't argue. It's true, and the business is profitable. Ask instead what would have to change — a second location, a handover, someone new at the counter — and leave the door open for that moment.

The fastest version: "If you handed this business to someone tomorrow, what would they struggle with that's currently in your head?"

"I don't have time to put all my products in."

Legitimate, and the biggest onboarding risk in this vertical. A style is the SKU with every colour beneath it, so catalogues are deep.

But the picture is better than it looks. Branded goods have manufacturer portals with published dealer pricing and live stock. Imported goods carry the supplier's own item numbers, stable enough to reorder against. Come with a plan for their specific suppliers, not a generic import promise.

"We tried a system once and it was a disaster."

Take it seriously — this industry has a real memory of it. A large regional distributor went bankrupt in 1990 after a new system left it unable to invoice, and its founder went on to build one of the biggest dealers in the state.

Answer with sequencing, not reassurance: start with one part of the business, keep the old process running alongside, and don't cut over everything at once.

"My installers will never use an app."

Correct, and don't fight it. They're subcontractors working for several dealers and have no reason to learn one store's tool. Work orders already go out by email — they used to go by text — so the format they're used to still works. Anything on a phone is an upgrade path, not a requirement.

"Send me some information."

This is a no. Email went zero-for-thirty in this research. Convert it to a time instead: "I'm back on this road Thursday — what's your slowest hour?"

The channel play

Where the leverage is, if it works

Direct field sales works, but the arithmetic is demanding. Six stores a day in a dense corridor, a realistic conversion from visit to conversation to demonstration to close, and one rep produces a modest number of accounts a year against a price ceiling that the field says sits around five thousand dollars.

That is not an argument against the market. It's an argument that the channel has to do the work the price can't.

Manufacturer and distributor reps

They already have weekly physical access to every store on the list, and they already carry free technology into those stores. A referral arrangement is standard in vertical software. Two caveats: reps are usually employed by the manufacturer, so the company has to sanction it, and a paid recommendation may be discounted by dealers once it's known to be paid.

Manufacturer subsidy

The more interesting version of the same idea. Manufacturers already pay for visualizers and sample stands that sit in dealer showrooms. Whether one would underwrite a management system for its dealer network is an untested but plausible question — and it would sidestep the price objection entirely.

Trade shows and press

Fund these as partnership development, measured on conversations with manufacturers and groups — not as lead generation. The dealer isn't there. Fund the Atlantic City market and in-store clinics as dealer acquisition instead.

Buying groups

Potentially the highest leverage of all, if groups mandate or subsidise software. Unverified — no group member was interviewed. Until someone asks, deprioritise it.

What to measure

Two numbers matter more than the rest

Cost per customer, split by channel. Track rep-referred separately from cold field visits from day one. The difference between those two numbers decides whether this vertical is a direct-sales business or a partnership business, and everything else follows from it.

Still running at ninety days. For a store that ran twenty-six years on paper, the risk isn't closing the deal — it's reverting to the filing cabinet in month two. Measure the share of accounts still transacting weekly at ninety days, and treat a fall there as more serious than a slow month of new sales.

Supporting: stores visited per field day, meeting-to-demonstration rate, days from signature to first live transaction, and share of the catalogue loaded at thirty days.
