Rundoo Flooring Market Insights

Rundoo serves independent supply stores across hardware, paint, lumber, farm and feed, and lawn and garden. This memo asks whether flooring is a viable next vertical — how the market works, who the incumbent software providers are, how independents compete, and where Rundoo would fit.

Research base: six interviews across the value chain plus desk research — a family owner (Royal Stone), two store managers (Carpet Place, Standard Tile), a 40-year veteran and former owner (West Carpets), a senior salesperson (Worldwide), and Huey Ho, a contractor and architect. 30+ businesses contacted; every substantive conversation came from walking in.

The short version. The independent flooring dealer is not choosing between Rundoo and a competing ERP. Most run on paper, and the one owner who priced flooring software declined it at $6–7K. The constraint is price and inertia, not features.

Market size

And the distinction most reports get wrong

17,834 US floor covering stores, $61.5bn in 2026. Separately there are 118,405 flooring installer businesses, which most market reports conflate with retailers. Installers hold no inventory and place no purchase orders — the addressable dealer count is roughly a tenth of headline figures. Growth is flat (under 1%) but evergreen. (IBISWorld)

Two differences matter. In paint, hardware and lumber the incumbents are horizontal (Epicor, ECI); in flooring they are flooring-native and consolidating. And flooring is the only one of these verticals where the store owns the installation labor.

How does the market work?

Project retail, not counter retail

Revenue starts with an in-home measurement and ends with an installation.

A note on terminology

The industry uses "wholesale" loosely. Three distinct things:

  • Importer-wholesalers — MSI, Soho Studio, TimeFloor. Buy from overseas factories, warehouse, and sell to retailers, contractors and homeowners directly.
  • Distributor-owned retail — Standard Tile sells at retail but is part of a distribution business, which is why it can see stock across five locations and into supplier systems.
  • Retailers trading under a wholesale name — Worldwide operates as "Worldwide Wholesale Floor Coverings" but is a five-location retailer. The name is positioning, not structure.

The chain gets bypassed constantly. Large contractors buy direct from manufacturers; wholesalers sell direct to homeowners; Home Depot's contractor membership offers credit, delivery and no sales tax. The retailer is optional at several points.

Residential

Runs ~1.5 weeks at ~$6,000 average, 5–6 customers a week, 80–85% close. Negotiation happens 90% of the time; on large jobs the store phones the manufacturer rep mid-quote for a discount and beats the competitor, winning roughly nine in ten.

Commercial

A different business. An architect specifies every finish, the package goes to four or five GCs, and the dealer bids to the GC as one sub among many. Projects run weeks to over a year and plans revise constantly — West Carpets had product specified in January discontinued by September.

Labor: three models across four stores

  • Subcontracted (Carpet Place, Worldwide) — ~15 installers paid per foot, never hourly. Pay is itemised by task: rip up, install, steps, furniture moves, wall base. Worldwide contracts out even the estimating.
  • W2 employees (West Carpets) — prevailing-wage public work requires it.
  • Mixed (Royal Stone) — five installers, estimators as a separate role.

Any system serving this market has to tolerate all three.

The store never bills the crew — the crew bills the store, in a batch, a week later. And the installer writes the invoice, not the store; payment goes by check. On larger jobs the store deals only with a crew boss, who brings his own four or five crews and pays them by the day.

Reps and samples

Manufacturer reps visit weekly or biweekly and are the primary source of industry news. They restock racks, pull discontinued boards, pitch new product, and negotiate spot discounts mid-quote. Asked how she hears about new technology, the store manager described a rep bringing in peel-and-stick tile. Reps also sell past the dealer entirely, calling on architects and interior designers directly.

Sample displays are bought, not given. The common model is that the dealer pays for a collection up front and is rebated once that collection hits a sales threshold — Carpet Place pays around $1,000 for 30–40 boards, refunded at roughly $5,000 in sales; Standard Tile describes the same structure. Genuinely free racks exist but can misfire: MSI supplied tile racks to Windsor Kitchen & Bath, which doesn't sell flooring, and per the owner nothing ever sold and the racks now sit unused. West Carpets dropped the program entirely. Worldwide takes the opposite position and keeps no samples at all. Nobody tracks samples as inventory.

Catalog and pricing access — varies by segment

For branded goods, manufacturers publish dealer pricing and live stock on B2B portals. Carpet Place checks the Dixie Group/Masland portal for unit price per square yard and availability while the customer is standing there; Standard Tile can see manufacturer stock by state for about half its suppliers.

For imported goods, there is no brand layer — Royal Stone works with several overseas factories and doesn't front their logos — but the product codes are the supplier's own item numbers, assigned by the factory and used by both sides to identify the exact product on reorder. A catalog exists; it just lives upstream.

For commercial, pricing arrives by email from the rep on a quantity basis, negotiated per project rather than listed.

The structural challenge is depth, not access: the style is the SKU with many colors beneath it, so a catalog needs style plus every color in the line.

The one thing that regularly changes a job mid-installation is an uneven floor, which can't be seen until the old carpet comes up.

What the field actually shows

  • Carpet Place — 100% paper. No POS, no ERP, no QuickBooks, no customer list. Orders filed in a cabinet by install date; install scheduling in a paper book. The only computer use is checking stock on mill portals.
  • Royal Stone — WeChat, WhatsApp, Gmail, Excel, QuickBooks. Open to software, but worried it can't keep pace with 1–2 month turnover.
  • Standard Tile — a real system (Cyncly, per their website), inventory visible across five stores.
  • Worldwide — declined to discuss, calling it proprietary.

The refusal is itself data. The dealers running on paper talked openly for 45 minutes; the five-location dealer with a real system treated its stack as competitive information.

The price ceiling — the most useful finding

Carpet Place's owner researched a flooring ERP and was quoted $6,000–7,000 upfront plus monthly. He declined on cost. QFloors lists $349/month for three users plus setup from $1,500. At ~$1.5–1.8M revenue, ~$5K/yr is 0.3% of sales — and he still said no.

This is not an owner refusing technology. He priced the category and made a capital decision against it.

A 40-year veteran put the segment plainly: most mom-and-pops have no system at all, and everything is still done by hand.

Software risk here is not abstract

Worldwide's founder, Alan Braunstein, previously ran Kane Carpet, a Secaucus distributor doing $90M a year. A $318,000 McDonnell Douglas system went live in March 1989; within days orders were being delivered multiple times and inventory records were wrong. Kane went bankrupt in 1990 owing $35M and sued for $37M; the vendor blamed the recession, and the verdict isn't in the public record. Braunstein founded Worldwide in 1991.

Source: NJBIZ, "Did Kane Carpet Die at the Hand of Customized Computer Software?" — named independently by Lynn at West Carpets.

Who are main competitors vs. independents?

Where the ground is actually being lost

Floor & Decor — 281 stores, 39 states, ~8% of the industry. But comparable store sales fell 2.1% while total sales rose 3.0%: all growth is from opening stores, not from existing ones. They sell no carpet. Michelle said Carpet Place dropped tile because it's hard to estimate — shape, diagonal layouts, edging — and now sends those customers to Home Depot or Floor & Decor, while keeping carpet. The categories big box takes are the ones that sell over a counter.

Independents compete on price and quality. Customers phone every store; dealers discount by volume, stock age and special groups, and ask manufacturers for job-specific discounts. One tile retailer stopped marking prices on displays altogether, because tariffs and fuel move costs quarterly. Standard Tile sells European tile and says some contractors refuse to install Floor & Decor's product. Worldwide's framing: they sell flooring material, we're a flooring company.

Showroom traffic has collapsed — Saturdays once brought ~50 customers, now ~10.

Buying groups (CCA Global, Abbey, Alliance, NFA) pool purchasing power and sometimes dictate technology. Carpet Place is not a member and had never heard of CCA, nor keeps contact with other owners. No buying-group member was interviewed — the largest gap in this research, and the question that would most change the go-to-market answer.

What would entering this market look like?

Two paths, requiring different amounts of building

Don't compete on breadth. Cyncly owns the enterprise tier. At the independent end the incumbent is paper and the competitor is inertia.

Path 1 — land where the existing product already fits

The speculative importer-retailer. Royal Stone and Tile Loft buy months ahead, run warehouses, clear stock monthly, and manage it in Excel. Inventory, purchasing, costs and catalogs are Rundoo's product today, and the catalog burden is lighter than expected — supplier item numbers are stable enough to reorder against. Royal Stone is explicitly open to software. Lowest build cost, fastest first customer.

Path 2 — build the project layer, where the larger segment sits

Special-order retailers like Carpet Place hold no stock, so inventory and purchasing have little to attach to. Their pain is measure, quote, scheduling and installer reconciliation — none of which Rundoo has. This is the bigger population and the harder build, and it's where the accompanying prototype is aimed.

The wedge that connects them

Carpet Place supplies every installation material except tackless strip — wall base, transition strips, glue, pads — billed manufacturer → store → customer, and its second location stocks moldings, metal and reducers because contractors need material same-day. That inventory behaves exactly like a paint or hardware store's, even at a dealer that otherwise carries nothing. And contractors are the repeat customers; homeowners rarely return, which makes charge accounts and customer records more valuable than dealers realise.

The timing

Cyncly–Broadlume closes in December. Overlapping ERPs get rationalised, which means forced migrations — dealers already absorbing switching costs are the cheapest customers a new entrant gets.

What would make me wrong

  • If dealers won't buy anything that doesn't own the job, Path 1 stalls and the real cost is the full project layer.
  • If Rundoo can't ingest from many small overseas suppliers as readily as from a handful of US mills, the importer segment gets harder.
  • If $6–7K was a proxy for "I don't want this," a lower price won't help.

Limitations

Six interviews, one metropolitan area, only one with an owner and by video. No buying-group member reached. No pain quantified in hours or dollars. Royal Stone notes are summaries, not a transcript.
